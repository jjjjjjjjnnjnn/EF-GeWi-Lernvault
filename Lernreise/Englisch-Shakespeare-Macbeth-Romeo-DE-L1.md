## Schritt 2 — entdecken

PRETRAINING (five core terms; read, cover, reproduce actively):

- Soliloquy (Soliloquium): a speech delivered alone on stage revealing inner thoughts to the audience.
- Aside (Aside): a brief remark to the audience that other characters on stage supposedly do not hear.
- Blank verse (Blankvers): unrhymed lines of roughly ten syllables, close to natural speech but rhythmically steady.
- Dramatic irony (dramatische Ironie): the audience knows something the characters do not, creating suspense or pity.
- Early Modern bridge (Fruehneuenglisch-Bruecke): thou means you (subject), thee means you (object), thy and thine mean your, hath means has, doth means does.

Klausur-Satz: `Soliloquy and aside give the audience privileged knowledge, which is the basis of dramatic irony.`

## Schritt 3 — entdecken

DISCOVERY (one concept plus diagram):

The eavesdropping structure runs: the audience always knows more than the stage. Soliloquy speaks the truth behind closed doors to the audience; aside passes a secret note with the door open; blank verse makes truth sound like a heartbeat (ten-syllable steady beat); dramatic irony watches characters fail with the answer in hand — we know the imagined blood never washes off while the sleepwalker keeps scrubbing. Work with paraphrase and invented lines only; no full-scene memorisation. Below only short public-domain quotes appear (first performed and printed over four hundred years ago, long out of copyright), each with a modern gloss.

```diagram
          AUDIENCE (knows most)
          ^                  ^
          | soliloquy        | aside ("you hear, they don't")
          |                  |
     [alone on stage]   [whisper to audience]
          |                  |
          +--------+---------+
                   |
            blank verse (heartbeat rhythm)
                   |
           dramatic irony = audience knows MORE
                    than the character
```

Klausur-Satz: `Privileged speech forms create an information gap between audience and character.`

## Anekdote & Fun-Fact

**Anekdote / Fun-Fact (EN)**: Actors call the sleepwalking scene a "nightmare solo": no dialogue partner, only a candle, a spot, and short broken lines. The shorter the line, the heavier the guilt — Shakespeare writes panic by breaking the verse.

**Bezug zum Konzept**: `Broken rhythm signals a broken mind: fragmented lines mirror fragmented conscience.`

## Schritt 4 — ausprobieren

MODEL TASK (worked example with solution):

[Werkzeug: highlighter]

TASK (analyze, AFB II): Analyse the public-domain mini-passage below (Macbeth, sleepwalking scene, heavily shortened, original spelling modernised). Mark soliloquy traits and one image, then write a P.E.E. chain.

Passage (public domain, short quotes plus modern paraphrase): Lady Macbeth, alone with a candle, rubs her hands: "Out, damned spot! out, I say!" [Modern: Get off, cursed stain!] "What is done cannot be undone." [Modern: What happened cannot be reversed.] "Will these hands ever be clean?" [Modern: Will my guilt ever wash away?]

HELP:
1. Fix the form — alone on stage plus candle plus self-talk marks soliloquy (no aside, no partner).
2. Mark the language — imperative "Out ... out", repetition, question "Will ...?", image "spot and hands".
3. Build the bridge — word check: "What is done" reads as a modern passive; "damned" means cursed. Write P.E.E.: Point (guilt), Evidence (eight words maximum), Explanation (effect).

MODEL SOLUTION: Point: The imperatives "Out, damned spot! out, I say!" expose lasting guilt. Evidence: doubled "out" plus exclamation. Explanation: She scrubs an invisible stain before the audience, so private remorse becomes visible; the spot image turns moral guilt into a physical mark that no washing removes. Point 2: The question "Will these hands ever be clean?" deepens the reading: its expected answer "never" admits that rank and power cannot undo murder. Vocabulary bridge: "damned" means cursed (modern), "spot" means stain and guilt-mark, "What is done cannot be undone" means the past is irreversible. Blank-verse note: the lines run short and broken, mirroring a disturbed mind rather than flowing reason.

Klausur-Satz: `The repeated imperatives turn invisible guilt into a visible stage image.`

## Schritt 5 — ausprobieren

COMPARISON (two procedures):

COMPARISON: Decide first — (i) analysis procedure (analyze: break down form plus quote plus effect, answering how it is written) or (ii) interpretation procedure (interpret: infer character and theme judgement, answering what it means) — then write.

TASK A: Analyze the function of the aside in an invented Romeo-style line: "[Aside] I smile at the feast, yet my heart kneels elsewhere."
TASK B: Interpret what the line reveals about love and loyalty.

HELP: A asks for the "function of the aside" -> procedure (i), form plus knowledge gap. B asks "what ... reveals" -> procedure (ii), reading plus theme. Rule: "function" and "how is X used" require analysis; "interpret", "what does X reveal" and "suggest about" require interpretation with paraphrase plus theme (love vs duty, appearance vs truth).

ANSWER: A requires procedure (i): The aside gives the audience secret knowledge — the smile is a public mask, the kneeling heart a private truth — heard only by us; this split creates dramatic irony for the following feast dialogue. B requires procedure (ii): The line suggests love as divided loyalty: social duty demands a smile, inner feeling demands devotion. It supports the theme that in both plays passion collides with an order (family honour, royal power) that cannot contain it.

Klausur-Satz: `While analysis describes the aside as a channel of secret knowledge, interpretation reads it as proof of divided loyalty.`
